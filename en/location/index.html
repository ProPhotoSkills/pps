LOCATION

Location is your technical compass when you head out with camera and gear.

Photography locations are much more than backgrounds. They determine the visual impact, the atmosphere and the entire energy of a shoot. Professional location scouting means selecting places deliberately, understanding them and integrating them into a visual concept.

In this category you learn how to find, analyse and make optimal use of the right shooting locations – from urban environments to natural settings. It is not only about aesthetics but about function: light, spatial effect, access, permits and brand fit.

Whether studio, outdoor or unusual off spaces – the right location often decides the image more than the technology.

Frequently asked questions about LOCATION

Choosing, reading and using locations strategically – from scouting basics to permits and safety.

What does the LOCATION section at ProPhotoSkills cover?
LOCATION covers the strategic selection, analysis and use of shooting locations. It is not only about aesthetics but about function: light, spatial effect, brand fit, permits, safety and budget. Whoever does not just use places but understands them gains a depth of image that technology alone cannot create. The category comprises 15 chapters – from scouting basics to cultural contexts and safety planning.
What is professional location scouting – and how does it differ from "location shopping"?
Location shopping looks for beautiful places. Professional scouting looks for the functionally right place for a specific story, a job, a moment. The difference begins with a single question: what does this place have to achieve – not: what should it look like? Only once function, spatial effect, depth and freedom of movement are defined does real scouting begin. Instagram and Pinterest are starting points, not a basis for decisions.
Why does every place have its own light – and how do I learn to read it?
Light is not a neutral resource. Every location has a specific light behaviour – depending on the time of day, orientation, reflective surfaces, architecture and vegetation. Professionals visit locations at different times of day and observe how the light moves. Whoever learns to read places according to their light potential instead of their surface appearance makes more strategic decisions – and later saves considerable time during set-up.
How does the location influence the brand impact of an image?
Every space communicates before a subject becomes visible. It carries attitude, pace, standards and ambition – read consciously or unconsciously. Brand photography therefore does not begin with the subject but with the question: does this space support the identity that is to be conveyed? One and the same person looks completely different depending on the location – without changing anything. Strong brand images follow a spatial logic, not chance.
Do I need permits for a photo shoot in public places?
Yes – often. Public accessibility does not automatically mean a right of use for commercial productions. Being physically present at a place is not the same as permission to shoot and exploit the images. Many shoots are "tolerated" – that is not a right and can be revoked at any time. Professionals consistently separate access, recording and exploitation and clarify permits in writing – especially for commercial use.
Indoor or outdoor – which location is better for which job?
The choice is not a question of style but a strategic decision. Indoor offers control over light, climate and workflow – ideal for complex production setups, product shots and time-critical jobs. Outdoor offers authenticity, natural light and spatial depth – but also variables such as weather, passers-by and limited infrastructure. Professionals first define the requirements of the job, then the location – not the other way round.
How do you secure an outdoor shoot in bad weather?
Weather is not a disruption but a property of every outdoor location. Professional planning does not work towards ideal conditions but towards realistic scenarios. That means: understanding the weather behaviour of the place in advance, recognising microclimate zones, planning rain alternatives as plan B and wording the briefing to clients realistically with regard to the weather. Anything that goes better is a bonus – not a prerequisite.
What is the difference between set design and a real location?
Authenticity is not a question of reality but of impact. A staged location can look more real than a real one – if it fits the message of the image better. The decisive question is not "What is real?" but "What has to look true in the image?". Set design gives complete control but requires more resources. Real locations deliver atmosphere and depth but demand more preparation and flexibility.
How do I immediately recognise the potential of an inconspicuous location?
Strong images often come about where others walk past. The potential of a place does not lie in how obvious it is but in its function: how does the light fall? What spatial depths are there? What textures, contrasts and axes of movement exist? Professionals think in requirements instead of motifs – they do not look for "beautiful places" but for spaces with the right variables for the planned image. Practice in "reading places" sharpens this eye – it can be trained.
What does location safety mean on professional shoots?
A location can be visually perfect – if it is unsafe, it is unsuitable. Safety already begins with the selection: accessibility, clarity, stability, infrastructure. As a leader, the photographer bears responsibility for crew, equipment and workflow. Professional planning defines working zones, axes of movement and responsibilities before the first set-up. Unclear locations are often not dangerous but simply not thought through – and that creates risks.
When is it worth using improvised backdrops such as backyards or off spaces?
Whenever the place carries the image that no classic location would deliver. Improvised backdrops – living rooms, backyards, stairwells, industrial wasteland – create authenticity and visual tension that staged sets can hardly replicate. The key: do not photograph despite the place, but through it. Whoever understands the space as a co-creator finds the strongest images in unexpected places – provided permits and safety have been clarified.
