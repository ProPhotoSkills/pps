Prejudice vs. experience

SKILL, T05_E04

Written by Eric P.

← All the coaches ....The client's view - when trust suddenly turns into budget →

Introduction

Why your photographic path only begins when you start to see for yourself 

Prejudices are like those small invisible pebbles in your shoe: not big enough to make you stop, but just annoying enough to remind you with every step that something is not right. As a photographer you have collected more of them than you realise. Some you inherited, some you picked up at workshops, some you snapped up on the internet because someone in a forum claimed loudly enough that it was the truth. And at some point, without noticing, you are walking around with a whole collection of them. The only question is: how much longer do you want to carry them with you? Because the path you block with prejudices is basically the same path that would actually take you further photographically and personally. And that is exactly what this essay is about: how you learn to free yourself from old ballast and make your own experience the strongest force in your development.

In the course of your photographic journey you will keep standing at crossroads where your prejudices whisper to you: "Left is dangerous", "Right won't work", "Straight ahead is safe, just stick with the old ways." But experience – this quiet, often far too modest teacher – says something else. It says: go. Try things out. Leave the predetermined track. And above all: make your own mistakes. Because mistakes are not the problem. They are the material a photographer is built from. Only prejudices want to make you believe you will get through more safely without mistakes. The joke is: nobody gets through without mistakes. Least of all in photography.

It is fascinating how early these inner assumptions arise. You pick up your first camera, press the shutter a few times and already your head starts telling you rules. "ISO is evil", "a photographer never shoots against the sun", "forget it with kit lenses", "real professionals only shoot with primes", "zooms are full of compromises", "you always have to shoot RAW", "available light is amateurish", "real portraits are only shot at 85mm." And at first you believe all of it, because it feels as if you have access to knowledge. But in truth it is only the beginning of a long series of mental stumbling blocks that ought to be called experience but are only assertions.

There are photographers who shape their entire career around prejudices without ever noticing how much they limit themselves. They avoid difficult lighting situations because someone said "harsh sun is useless". They avoid photographing people because they believe they are not communicative enough. They do not try new genres because they believe you have to specialise. They hate flash, although they never really learnt to master it. And they withdraw from jobs because they believe they are "not good enough", although exactly these projects could give them the decisive experience.

I'll tell you something you may only grasp at second glance: prejudices are like fog. They take away your view without touching you. They are there, but they are not real. Experience, on the other hand, is wind. Experience brings movement, clarity, visibility. It drives the fog away instead of ignoring it. You don't need more light – you need movement. And that is exactly the difference between a photographer who stands still and a photographer who grows.

You know this feeling: you see a photographer who seems to work effortlessly. Who stays calm even when the light is difficult or the client seems nervous. You sense the composure, that kind of "All good, we'll sort this out." And you ask yourself: what does he have that I don't? The answer is almost always the same: experience. Not talent, not equipment, not magic. Experience. And experience is nothing other than the sum of mistakes, successes, surprises, disappointments, coincidences, human encounters, moments of light and situations that challenged you. And that is exactly why experience is not only professionally valuable but also personally transformative.

Because if we are honest, photography is much less technique than psychology. It is not the camera that decides how good your image will be, but your ability to read the situation. Your ability to stay calm. Your ability to see. And you can only see if you let go of what you think you know. A prejudice tells you what you expect. An experience shows you what really happens. And those are two completely different universes.

Perhaps you are asking yourself: why are prejudices so powerful? Why are they so present, although they limit us? The answer lies in the nature of our brain. The brain loves shortcuts. It loves patterns. It loves the feeling of knowing something before it happens. Control is a strong psychological need. And prejudices give you the feeling of control – even when they are wrong. Experience, on the other hand, takes this sense of security away from you because it forces you to really live through situations. It is more unpredictable, more honest, more direct. It shows you that you do not know everything after all. And that is exactly what makes it more strenuous – but also more valuable.

When you engage with experience, you leave the comfort zone of presumed truth and enter the reality of actual experience. That not only makes you a better photographer but also more stable as a person. You learn to deal with uncertainty. You learn that things do not always go perfectly. You learn that light does not stick to rules. You learn that people do not work according to a pattern. And you learn that the moment is always bigger than your planning.

I remember countless situations on set in which photographers panicked because something did not go according to plan: clouds in front of the sun, model too shy, client undecided, location smaller than expected after all. Those who let themselves be guided by prejudices quickly lose their nerve in such moments. They cling to their mental rules and discover that reality does not stick to them. Those who have experience, however, smile quietly, shrug their shoulders and say: "Okay, then we'll do it differently." And exactly this differently is often the moment when magic happens.

Because experience encourages creativity. Prejudices prevent it.

Experience says: "What happens if I set the light completely differently?"
Prejudice says: "Hard light doesn't work."

Experience says: "I'll just try this perspective."
Prejudice says: "That's not done."

Experience says: "The client is nervous – how can I relax him?"
Prejudice says: "The client interferes too much."

Experience says: "Every model has a side that shines."
Prejudice says: "The model is difficult."

Experience is flexible. Prejudices are rigid.
Experience is curious. Prejudices are focused on safety.
Experience is honest. Prejudices are comfortable.

Experience is a process. Prejudices are a state.

And now comes the decisive point: photography lives from the process, not from the state. No image is static. No moment repeats itself. No light stays the same. No person is predictable. And that is exactly why experience is so indispensable. You have to learn to dance, not just to stand. You have to learn to react, not just to plan. You have to learn to adapt to situations, not to fight them.

Again and again I meet young photographers who believe they can shorten the path of learning by collecting knowledge. But knowledge is not experience. Knowledge is a toolbox. Experience is the craftsman. Knowledge is potential. Experience is real. Knowledge tells you how it should be. Experience shows you how it is.

Perhaps the biggest mistake many photographers make is assuming that they should wait until they are ready. They wait for more talent. They wait for better equipment. They wait for more knowledge. But you don't become ready by waiting. You become ready by doing. And that is exactly why experience is the key. It is not about how well prepared you are. It is about how open you are to learning.

You can watch the best tutorials in the world, you can attend every workshop, you can buy the most expensive camera – but if you are not willing to throw your prejudices overboard and step into reality, you will not grow. It is like swimming: you can't learn to swim by sitting at the edge of the pool watching videos. At some point you have to get into the water. And at the beginning you will swallow water. That is unavoidable. But that is the only way you learn to swim.

Experience is not just a teacher – it is a mirror. It shows you not only the world, it shows you yourself. You will be surprised how often your prejudices say less about the outside world than about your own fears. Prejudices like "I can't direct a model" are often just the fear of closeness. "I can't do flash" is often just the fear of technology. "I can't be confident in front of clients" is often just the fear of being judged. Experiences do not destroy these fears immediately – but they make them smaller, because you learn that they do not determine reality.

Photography is a game between you and the world. Prejudices make this game smaller, experience makes it bigger. The bigger your room to play, the greater your ability. And the greater your ability, the greater your self-confidence. And the greater your self-confidence, the more freedom you have in the creative process.

But one thing is decisive: experience only comes about if you are willing to give up control a little. You have to allow things to go differently. You have to be willing to be surprised. You have to accept that you don't know everything. And you have to understand that exactly in this not-knowing lies the space for growth. A prejudice gives you the feeling of knowing. An experience gives you the opportunity to understand.

The more experiences you have, the stronger your photographic compass becomes. And this compass is priceless. It guides you through difficult sets. It helps you understand clients better. It lets you read light like a language. It gives you calm in stressful moments. It makes you more efficient. And above all: it makes you more authentic.

Authenticity is what clients sense. People don't book you because of your camera. They book you because they sense that you know what you are doing. And this knowledge does not come from a book. It comes from experiences. It comes from real moments. It comes from situations you have mastered.

And now imagine how much your photographic path would change if you lived the next year not by prejudices but by experiences. How many new ideas you would develop. How many new people you would get to know. How many new skills you would build. How many new subjects you would discover. How many new perspectives you would gain.

Prejudices hold you back because they tell you what is not possible. Experience moves you forward because it shows you everything that is possible.

Inside you there is a photographer who is far bigger than the one you know today. This photographer does not wait for rules. This photographer tries things out. This photographer sees more. This photographer trusts himself. And this photographer only comes about through one thing: through experience.

There will be moments when you doubt. Moments when you think you are making no progress. Moments when prejudices knock again and say: "Better stay here, it's safe." But I promise you: every step into experience is worth it. Every step takes you a little further away from fear and brings you a little closer to yourself.

In the end it is quite simple: prejudices protect your ego. Experiences strengthen your personality. Prejudices give you a feeling of security. Experiences give you real freedom. Prejudices whisper to you how the world supposedly works. Experiences show you how it really works.

And that is exactly where your next stage lies. Your next level. Not in technical knowledge. Not in equipment. But in your willingness to open yourself to the world instead of filtering it through ready-made thoughts.

Prejudices are the beginning.
Experience is the path.
You are the photographer who decides which one you take.
