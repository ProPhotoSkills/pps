Frequently asked questions about TRAVEL

Travel logistics for professional photo productions – from buffer time to customs.

What does the TRAVEL section at ProPhotoSkills cover?
TRAVEL covers the entire travel logistics of professional photo productions – from buffer times and the choice of transport to packing equipment, customs and visas and cultural codes abroad. The principle: logistics protects creativity. Whoever plans the journey professionally arrives on set calm and focused – and can lead from the very first minute.
Why are buffer times so important in photo productions?
Creativity needs space – whoever arrives on set in a rush has neither the head nor the energy for good images. A buffer protects against delays and technical breakdowns, gives time for deliberately exploring the location and makes it possible to get the team in the mood calmly. Buffer time is not wasted time but the invisible investment in composure and image quality.
Plane, train or car – what is the right means of transport for photographers?
The answer depends on distance, the amount of equipment and the time budget. The plane is hardly avoidable over long distances, but brings weight limits and customs risks. The train allows more luggage at no extra cost and is often more relaxed over medium distances. The car offers maximum flexibility with large amounts of equipment. Professionals choose the means of transport strategically – not out of habit.
What always has to be in a photographer's carry-on?
Everything that is irreplaceable: camera body, the most important lens, laptop, external hard drives, memory cards and batteries. Airlines compensate for lost checked luggage with money – but no compensation, however high, replaces lost images or prevents a cancelled shooting day. The basic rule: pack your carry-on so that you could still work in an emergency.
How do you transport photo equipment safely when travelling?
Hard cases like Pelican or HPRC are a must for air travel – indestructible, waterproof and lockable. Trolleys with camera compartments are perfect for carry-on luggage. For larger productions: distribute accessories among several crew members, check each airline's weight limits in advance and always carry batteries in your hand luggage – lithium-ion batteries are not allowed in the hold.
What do photographers need to consider regarding visas and customs?
Tourist visas do not allow paid work – not even for a one-day commercial shoot. Anyone caught on a job with a tourist visa risks an entry ban, fines and losing the client. Depending on the country, work visas have to be applied for 4–6 weeks in advance. At customs: always declare equipment correctly, document serial numbers and carry an equipment list with values.
How do you deal with jet lag as a photographer?
Jet lag is an underestimated production factor. An overtired photographer makes worse decisions, loses control of the set and radiates no presence. Professionals plan arrival days with a sleep buffer, adjust their rhythm to the destination time zone before the trip, avoid alcohol on the plane and use the arrival time deliberately for calmly exploring the location.
What do cultural codes mean for a shoot abroad?
Every country is a different set – with unspoken rules that decide success or failure. In Japan punctuality means respect, in Brazil warmth counts more than a schedule, in conservative countries clothing is a visible professional signal. Photographers who know and respect cultural codes build trust faster – with local teams, models and clients. One hour of research can prevent days of misunderstandings.
Travel light or full gear – when do you travel with minimal equipment?
Travel light is suitable for commissions with a clearly defined scope, local equipment rental on site or travel to countries with strict customs regulations. Full gear is necessary for productions without local rental options, with special equipment or when client briefings require particular technology. The decision is not philosophy but calculation – and should be part of production planning.
How do you use travel time creatively – even away from the set?
Photography does not begin on set – it begins with the eye. On the train, in the taxi, at the airport: whoever has learnt to observe on the road sharpens their eye for light, composition and the moment. New places break up habitual ways of seeing and open the eye to visual languages that are not visible in everyday life. Travel as a source of inspiration is no coincidence – it can be trained.
